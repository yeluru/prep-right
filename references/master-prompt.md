PREFACE AND JOB DESCRIPTION SECTIONS
These two sections appear in the document immediately after the cover
page, before any analysis or research content. They are the reader's
orientation before the preparation work begins.

Preface:
Write a preface of three to four paragraphs addressed directly to the
candidate. It must do the following in order:

  - Open with a plain-English explanation of what this role actually is,
    written for someone who has not yet read the job description. Not a
    summary of the JD. A human explanation of the job: what the person
    will own, who they will lead, and what success looks like in year one.
    Make it concrete and specific to this company and role.

  - Explain how to use this guide. Tell the candidate the order in which
    to read it, how long each section will take, and which sections to
    prioritize if time is short. Be prescriptive, not open-ended.

  - Set the right mindset. Tell the candidate what this guide will and
    will not do for them. It will not give them answers to memorize. It
    will give them a way of thinking about the role that makes every
    question easier to navigate. One short, direct paragraph is enough.

The preface must be written in the same voice as the rest of the guide:
direct, specific, and occasionally surprising. Not motivational. Not
corporate.

Job Description:
Reproduce the full job description verbatim, formatted as a clean,
readable section of the document. Apply the following formatting:

  - Section title: "The Job Description" in a navy shaded header block.
  - The company name and role title as a gold-colored subheading.
  - The full JD text in body font, with any existing section headers
    (Responsibilities, Qualifications, About the Role, etc.) rendered
    as bold navy subheadings within the section.
  - Do not edit, paraphrase, or summarize the JD. Reproduce it exactly.
  - Place this section on its own page, after the preface and before
    the Company Intelligence Brief.

PRE-WORK: COMPANY AND ROLE INTELLIGENCE RESEARCH
Before executing any of the six steps below, conduct live web research
on the company and role. This is mandatory, not optional. Use web search
and web fetch to retrieve current, specific information from the following
sources. Do not rely on training knowledge alone, as it may be outdated.

Research targets:

  1. Company website: Read the About page, Leadership page, and any
     published technology or engineering blog. Identify the company's
     stated mission, strategic priorities, and any recent platform or
     product announcements relevant to the role.

  2. Glassdoor: Search for recent interview reviews for this company
     and this role or similar roles. Identify recurring interview
     question themes, interview process structure (number of rounds,
     panel composition, technical screen format), and any reported
     culture signals or red flags candidates have noted.

  3. LinkedIn: Search for current and recent employees in this role
     or adjacent roles at this company. Note their backgrounds,
     career trajectories, and any public posts or articles that reveal
     how the team talks about its work.

  4. Recent news: Search for the company name plus relevant terms
     (engineering blog, technology strategy, AI investment, recent
     layoffs or hiring, earnings calls, product launches). Identify
     anything that would give the candidate informed context about
     where the company is heading and what pressures the team is
     operating under.

  5. Engineering or tech blog: If the company publishes engineering
     content, find and read at least two recent posts relevant to
     the role's technical domain. Note specific technologies,
     architectural patterns, or engineering values the company
     publicly espouses.

  6. Competitive candidate profiling: Using LinkedIn, search for people
     who currently hold or recently held this exact role at this company
     and at its closest competitors. Note their educational backgrounds,
     prior company names, years of experience, and any skills or
     certifications that appear repeatedly. This is the profile of the
     person the candidate is competing against. Identify specifically
     where the candidate's background is stronger than the typical
     profile and where it is weaker. These observations feed directly
     into the Competitive Context section of the document.

Synthesize all research findings into a Company Intelligence Brief that
appears as the first section of the document, before Step 1. The brief
must include: what the company actually does (not the PR version), the
current strategic context the hiring manager is operating in, what
Glassdoor reveals about how this company interviews, and one or two
specific facts the candidate can reference in the interview to signal
they did their homework. Cite the sources used.

STEP 1 — DECODE THE ROLE
Read the job description as if you are the hiring manager who wrote it.
Incorporate what you learned from the company research to enrich this
analysis. Do not summarize the JD. Explain in plain conversational
English what problem the company is actually trying to solve by hiring
this person, given what you now know about the company's current
strategic context. Describe the three worlds this leader must operate
in simultaneously. Identify the specific KPIs or business outcomes this
role will be held accountable for. End with one sentence that captures
what separates an average candidate from the person who actually wins
this role.

---

STEP 2 — FIT ANALYSIS
Map the person's background against every requirement in the job
description. Be honest. For each requirement, state clearly whether this
is a strength, a partial match, or a real gap. Rate each requirement from
1 to 10. Render the complete fit analysis as a formatted scoring table.

After the scoring table, do the following for every requirement rated 7
or below. Do not soften this section. Do not frame gaps diplomatically.
Say directly what the problem is and exactly what the candidate must do
about it before the interview.

For each gap, write a dedicated Gap Briefing block that contains all
four of the following elements. Do not skip any element for any gap:

  1. The Blunt Assessment: One or two sentences that say plainly what
     is missing and why it is a problem for this specific role. Do not
     use phrases like "there is an opportunity to develop" or "this area
     could be strengthened." Say what is actually true. Example: "You
     have never worked in a payments company. Every interviewer in this
     process has spent years in payments and will probe this within the
     first five minutes. If you cannot speak credibly to how payment
     authorization infrastructure works, this gap will end your candidacy
     before the technical rounds begin."

  2. The Specific Preparation Plan: A step-by-step plan telling the
     candidate exactly what to do, read, build, or practice before the
     interview to close this gap as much as possible. Be concrete.
     Name specific resources, frameworks, concepts, or projects. Assume
     the candidate has one to two weeks. Do not give a generic reading
     list. Give a sequenced plan with a clear priority order.

  3. The Bridge Story: Identify the closest experience in the candidate's
     background that partially addresses this gap. Write the exact
     framing they should use to bridge from what they have done to what
     the role requires. This is not a fabrication. It is an honest
     translation of real experience into the relevant context.

  4. The Interview Landmine: Identify the single most dangerous question
     an interviewer could ask that would expose this gap fully. Write it
     out as the interviewer would ask it. Then write the model answer the
     candidate should give: an answer that is honest about the gap,
     demonstrates that they have done the work to understand the domain,
     and connects their existing experience to the requirements of the
     role without overclaiming.

Render each Gap Briefing block as a visually distinct section in the
document using a red left-border callout box for the Blunt Assessment,
followed by clearly labeled subsections for the Preparation Plan, Bridge
Story, and Interview Landmine. These blocks must be impossible to skim
past. They are the most important content in the document for candidates
who have real gaps.

End the section with an overall verdict: how competitive is this person
overall, and what are the two or three highest-priority actions they must
complete before the interview. Be direct about whether this person is
a strong contender, a borderline candidate, or someone who needs
significant preparation work before they should be walking into this room.

---

STEP 3 — MASTER PLAN AND 8 KNOWLEDGE DOMAINS
Identify the 8 knowledge domains this interview will probe, informed by
both the job description and the Glassdoor interview intelligence you
gathered. For each domain, do all of the following:

  - Explain the concept in plain English using a simple analogy.
    Assume the reader is intelligent but not yet an expert in this area.
  - Describe exactly why this domain matters for this specific role
    at this specific company, using what you learned in your research.
  - Map the person's background to this domain honestly.
  - Provide 2 to 3 specific data points or industry statistics that
    will make the candidate sound authoritative when speaking about it.
  - Write the exact framing the candidate should use when an interviewer
    probes this domain. Start this with the words "When they ask about
    this, open with..."

Every explanation must create a light bulb moment. If a sentence could
appear in any generic AI article, rewrite it until it could only appear
in this specific preparation guide.

---

STEP 4 — STORY ARSENAL
For each of the 8 knowledge domains, write a story card using the CERT
structure: Context (what was the situation and what made it hard),
Engineering or Action (what specifically did the person do — not the
team, the person), Result (what changed and what was measured), Tie Back
(the explicit one-sentence bridge connecting this story to what the
target role requires).

Draw only from the background provided. If there is no direct experience
for a domain, write placeholder guidance that tells the person exactly
what kind of story to find and how to frame it.

---

STEP 5 — MOCK INTERVIEW Q&A
Before the three interview layers, write a standalone opening section
titled "The Question Every Interview Starts With." This section contains
one question and one model answer only, and it must appear first, before
any of the three layers.

The question is: "Tell me about yourself."

This is not a warm-up question. It is the first impression, the framing
device, and the moment the candidate either takes control of the narrative
or surrenders it. Treat it with the same seriousness as any technical
question.

The model answer for this question must do all of the following:

  - Open with a single sentence that positions the candidate at the
    level of the role, not below it. Not "I have been in software
    engineering for 20 years." Something that immediately establishes
    relevance to this specific company and role.

  - Tell the career arc in 60 to 90 seconds of spoken English. Not a
    resume recitation. A narrative with a through-line: where they
    started, what they built, what they led, and why this role is the
    logical next move, not a lateral step.

  - Include one specific, quantified accomplishment that is directly
    relevant to what this company needs. Name the number. Name the
    impact. Do not be vague.

  - End with a forward-facing sentence that connects the candidate's
    trajectory to this role and this company specifically, using what
    was learned in the company research phase. This signals genuine
    interest and preparation, not a generic pitch.

  - The entire answer must be speakable in 90 seconds or less.
    Write it as spoken language, not prose. Short sentences. No
    subordinate clauses that take three beats to resolve. The candidate
    should be able to say this answer out loud without it sounding
    like they are reading.

  - After the model answer, include a coaching note that explains the
    structural choices made: why the opening line was written that way,
    what the arc accomplishes, and what the candidate should watch out
    for when delivering it (common delivery mistakes for this specific
    type of background).

Then write 4 questions and model answers for each of the 3 interview
layers below. Use the Glassdoor and company research to ground the
questions in the actual interview format and recurring themes reported
for this company, not generic leadership questions.

  Layer 1 — Technical Deep Dive (asked by engineering leaders)
  Layer 2 — Business Translation (asked by operations or business execs)
  Layer 3 — Leadership and Culture (asked by the hiring manager or HR)

Each model answer must demonstrate the right opening frame, the right
level of technical or business specificity, and a closing sentence that
connects the answer back to the role. Answers should sound like a
confident human conversation, not a rehearsed speech.

---

STEP 6 — 30-60-90 DAY VISION
Write a detailed 30-60-90 day plan for this specific role, grounded in
what you learned about the company's current priorities and challenges.
For each phase, describe the primary focus, list 5 specific activities,
and explain what the organization will have or be able to do differently
as a result of this phase. End with the exact language the candidate
should use to present this vision in the final minutes of the interview.

---

STEP 7 — QUESTIONS TO ASK THE INTERVIEWERS
At the Director level, the questions a candidate asks are evaluated as
carefully as the answers they give. An interviewer who has spent years
building the platform this role leads will notice immediately whether
the questions signal genuine curiosity and strategic thinking or whether
they signal someone going through the motions.

This section is not a courtesy list. It is a prepared offensive strategy.

For each of the three interview layers below, write five to eight
high-leverage questions the candidate should have ready. For each
question, do all three of the following:

  - Write the question exactly as the candidate should ask it, in
    natural spoken language. Not vague. Not generic. Specific enough
    that a weak answer from the interviewer tells the candidate
    something important about the role or the organization.

  - Write the reasoning behind the question: what the candidate is
    actually trying to learn, what a strong answer sounds like versus
    a weak one, and what a weak answer should signal to the candidate
    about whether they want this role.

  - Note whether the question is primarily informational (the candidate
    genuinely needs the answer), evaluative (the candidate is assessing
    the interviewer or the org), or positioning (the question itself
    demonstrates the candidate's thinking and signals they already
    belong at this level). Most of the best questions do all three.

Layer 1 questions for engineering leaders: Focus on platform health,
architectural debt, team capability gaps, how technical decisions are
made and reviewed, what the biggest unsolved engineering problems are,
and how the organization responds to production incidents. Avoid
questions about tech stack that Google could answer.

Layer 2 questions for business and operations executives: Focus on
how engineering success is measured from a business perspective, what
the relationship between product and engineering looks like in practice,
what the biggest business risks the platform currently carries, and
how investment priorities are set. These questions should signal that
the candidate thinks in business outcomes, not engineering outputs.

Layer 3 questions for the hiring manager and HR: Focus on what success
looks like at 90 days and 12 months, what the last person in this role
did well and where they fell short, how the hiring manager operates and
what they expect from their Directors, and what the honest obstacles are
that the person stepping into this role will face in the first six months.
At least one question should be direct enough to be slightly uncomfortable,
because it signals the candidate is serious about evaluating the role,
not just trying to get it.

After all questions are written, include a short coaching note on
question delivery: when to ask which questions, how many to ask in a
single round, and the one thing candidates almost always do wrong when
asking questions at the end of an interview.

STEP 8 — THE HARD QUESTIONS
These are the questions most candidates secretly hope will not come up.
They always come up. The candidate who has thought through these before
the interview is not just better prepared; they are visibly more
self-aware, and self-awareness is what senior leadership interviewers
are specifically evaluating.

Write a dedicated preparation section for each of the following five
question types. For each one, provide all four elements below. Do not
generalize. Every answer must be built specifically from this candidate's
background and this role's context.

  1. "Why are you leaving your current role?"
     The trap: any answer that sounds negative about a current employer,
     that sounds like a rehearsed deflection, or that fails to create a
     logical narrative arc from where the candidate is to where they are
     going. Write the model answer that is honest, forward-facing, and
     specific enough to be credible without being a liability. Then write
     a coaching note on what makes candidates blow this question and how
     to avoid it.

  2. "Why this company, and why this role specifically?"
     The trap: a generic answer that could apply to any company in the
     sector, or an answer that sounds like the candidate read the About
     page and nothing else. Use the company research from the pre-work
     phase to write a model answer that references at least two specific,
     non-obvious things the candidate learned about this company that
     made them want to be here rather than somewhere else. This answer
     must be impossible to give without genuine preparation.

  3. "Tell me about your biggest engineering leadership failure."
     The trap: choosing a failure that is not really a failure (the
     classic "I work too hard" equivalent), or oversharing in a way
     that raises more concerns than it resolves. Write the structural
     framework for answering this question well: what kind of failure
     to choose, how to describe it without catastrophizing, what the
     self-awareness component must demonstrate, and how to close it
     in a way that shows the candidate learned something transferable.
     Then write a model answer drawn from the candidate's actual
     background.

  4. "What would your last team say is your biggest weakness as a leader?"
     The trap: deflection, false modesty, or a weakness that sounds
     disqualifying for this role. Write the framework for choosing the
     right weakness to disclose: one that is genuine, one that is not
     a core competency of the role, and one that the candidate has
     actively worked to address with a specific result. Then write the
     model answer.

  5. Objection handling: "We are concerned that you do not have direct
     experience in [domain]."
     This is the most dangerous moment in any senior interview. An
     interviewer raising a direct concern is giving the candidate an
     opportunity, not issuing a verdict. Write the exact framework for
     responding to a direct objection about a gap: acknowledge it
     directly without shrinking, bridge to the closest relevant
     experience, demonstrate that you understand why the gap matters
     in this role, and make a specific commitment to how you will close
     it in the first 90 days. Then write a model answer for the most
     likely objection this specific candidate will face based on the
     fit analysis in Step 2.

Render each of the five hard question sections with a distinct visual
treatment in the document: a dark header block for the question, then
clearly labeled subsections for the trap, the framework, and the model
answer.

COMPETITIVE CONTEXT
This section appears after the Company Intelligence Brief and before
Step 1. It is built from the LinkedIn competitive candidate profiling
research conducted in the pre-work phase.

Write a two to three paragraph analysis that tells the candidate exactly
who they are competing against for this role. Be specific. Use what
the research revealed about the backgrounds of people who currently
hold or recently held this role. Cover the following:

  - What the typical competing candidate looks like: where they have
    worked, what their career arc looks like, what qualifications they
    almost always carry, and what level of experience is the norm
    versus the exception for this role at this company.

  - Where this candidate is stronger than the typical profile. Be
    specific. Name the experiences, accomplishments, or capabilities
    that differentiate them from the field. These are the things the
    candidate should find natural opportunities to surface during the
    interview, not because they are bragging but because they represent
    genuine signal that the interviewer should weight.

  - Where this candidate is weaker than or different from the typical
    profile. Be honest. These are the areas where the interviewer is
    most likely to probe, and knowing they are coming changes how the
    candidate prepares.

  - One paragraph of explicit positioning advice: given the competitive
    field, what is the single narrative the candidate should own in this
    interview that makes them the most memorable candidate in the pool?
    Not a slogan. A genuine positioning statement grounded in what makes
    this candidate's combination of experiences unusual or valuable for
    this specific role.

Render this section with a Competitive Landscape Summary Table showing
the typical competing candidate profile versus this candidate's profile,
side by side, with a column for each dimension (education, company
background, years of experience, domain expertise, AI and emerging tech,
leadership scale) and a clear visual indicator of where the candidate
leads, matches, or trails the field.

MULTI-ROUND INTERVIEW STRATEGY
Director-level roles at companies like this one typically run four to
six rounds. Each round has a different evaluator, a different focus,
and a different information state about the candidate. A candidate who
treats every round identically is leaving points on the table.

Write a round-by-round strategy guide for this specific role and company,
using what the Glassdoor research revealed about the interview process
structure. Cover the following for each round:

  - Who is likely in the room and what they care about most.
  - What the candidate should aim to establish or reinforce in this round
    that they could not have established in the previous one.
  - How to build on what landed well in prior rounds without repeating it.
  - How to address anything that did not land well in prior rounds without
    reopening it unnecessarily.
  - The one thing that typically goes wrong in this round for strong
    candidates and how to avoid it.

Include a specific section on continuity management: how to keep a
consistent narrative arc across all rounds so that interviewers who
compare notes afterward see a coherent picture of the candidate, not
six different versions of the same person. Include guidance on what to
do if an interviewer in a later round asks a question that was already
answered well in a prior round: how to give a fresh response that builds
on rather than repeats the prior answer.

Render the round-by-round strategy as a structured table with one row
per round, followed by a continuity management prose section.

POST-INTERVIEW STRATEGY
The interview does not end when the candidate leaves the room. At the
senior leadership level, the follow-up is a continuation of the
evaluation, and most candidates treat it as an administrative formality.

Write a post-interview action plan that covers all of the following:

  - Within 24 hours: Who to send a follow-up note to, what it should
    say, and what it should not say. The note should not be a generic
    thank-you. It should do at least one of the following: reinforce
    the candidate's strongest moment from the interview with a specific
    callback, address something that did not land as cleanly as intended
    with a concise and confident clarification, or add a piece of
    relevant context that the candidate wishes they had surfaced in the
    room. Write a model follow-up note for this specific role and
    candidate, personalized to the hiring manager layer. Write a
    separate shorter version for other panel members.

  - If there is silence after the expected response window: How long to
    wait before following up, what to say, and how to stay visible
    without appearing anxious. Write the exact language for a
    check-in message that signals confidence rather than desperation.

  - If an offer comes: What to do in the first 48 hours before
    responding. What to negotiate, how to negotiate it, and what not
    to negotiate. Use the compensation benchmark from Step 2 to frame
    the negotiation context. Write the model negotiation opener.

  - If there is a rejection: What to do, what to say, and how to
    keep the door open for future opportunities at this company without
    being unprofessional. At the Director level, the people who
    interviewed the candidate will cross paths with them again. How
    the candidate handles a rejection is part of their professional
    reputation.

INTERVIEW DAY REFERENCE CARD
This is the final page of the document. It must fit on a single page.
It is the only page the candidate needs to read the morning of the
interview. Design it as a visually distinct, high-density reference
card using a dark navy background with white and gold text for maximum
visual contrast and readability.

The card must contain exactly the following, and nothing else:

  - Opening line: The exact first sentence of the "tell me about
    yourself" answer, reproduced verbatim.

  - Positioning statement: Three sentences that capture who this
    candidate is, what they have built, and why they are the right
    person for this role. Written to be read silently for 20 seconds
    before walking into the building.

  - Five story anchors: The title of each of the five most important
    CERT stories, each with a single-line summary of the result.
    Format: Story name: one-line result. Five rows, no more.

  - Three company facts to drop: The two or three most specific,
    non-obvious facts from the company research that the candidate
    can reference naturally in conversation to signal genuine
    preparation. One line each.

  - Top two gap reminders: The two most important gap areas from the
    fit analysis, each with a single-line reminder of the bridge story
    to use. This is the candidate's mental flag to stay prepared when
    those topics surface.

  - The one question to end on: The single highest-leverage question
    from Step 7 that the candidate should ask in the final round if
    given the chance to ask only one. One line.

Do not add prose to this page. No explanations. No coaching notes.
The candidate has read the full guide. This page is the pre-game card.
Render it as a full-page designed block using dark navy shading, white
body text, gold section labels, and a clean two-column or structured
grid layout within the page margins.

FORMATTING AND TONE RULES — follow these without exception:

Write in flowing prose, not bullets, except where a table or structured
list genuinely serves the reader better than prose would.

Every paragraph must earn its place. If a sentence does not create
understanding, add nuance, or move the reader forward, cut it.

Never use em dashes. Use commas, colons, or periods instead.

Write as if you are talking to a smart person who is short on time and
high on stakes. Engaging, direct, and occasionally surprising. Not
corporate. Not academic. Not a textbook.

Use tables for comparisons, frameworks, and structured data. Every
table must have a clear header row and alternating row treatment for
readability.

When explaining a technical concept, always follow this sequence:
plain English explanation first, analogy second, why it matters for
this role third. Never start with the definition.

The language should never sound like you are teaching vocabulary.
It should sound like someone who already knows this world is bringing
the reader inside it.

Assume the reader knows nothing about the role or the domain going in.
Assume they will know everything about it by the time they finish reading.
